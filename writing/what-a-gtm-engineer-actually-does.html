What a GTM engineer actually does

Not quite sales ops, not quite data engineering. The plain-English version of the job, and what a real week of it looks like.

Alex RiveraNov 16, 20267 min read

When I tell people I’m a GTM engineer, I usually get one of two reactions. Engineers assume I work on sales software. Salespeople assume I’m the person who fixes the CRM, the database their whole team lives in. Both are a little bit right, and both miss what the job is really for.

GTM stands for go-to-market: everything a company does to find customers, reach them and win them. For most of the history of sales, that work ran on people. Someone researched accounts by hand, someone copied data from one tool into another, and someone decided each morning who to call. A GTM engineer builds the systems that do that work automatically, and does it well enough that the people can spend their time on the one part only people can do, which is the conversation.

If a sales rep is a driver, I build the car, the roads and the dashboard.

The machine

The easiest way to explain the job is to follow one company through the thing I build.

It starts as a name on a list. On its own that name is close to useless, so the first thing the system does is enrich it. It pulls in who works there, how big the company is, which tools they already use and whether anything has changed lately. Most of that comes from outside data providers, and a surprising amount of the craft is in combining several of them, so the gaps in one are covered by another.

Good data still doesn’t tell you when to reach out, so the next layer watches for signals. A new head of sales joins. The company raises money. Someone spends ten minutes on our pricing page. When one of those happens, the account goes to the right person with the reason attached, while it still matters.

Then comes the outreach itself, which is the part most people picture when they hear about this job. I build the sequences and the plumbing behind them, but the goal is the opposite of volume.

The job isn’t sending more emails. It’s making sure fewer, better ones go to the right people at the right moment.

The last piece is measurement. Every step produces numbers, and I turn them into a small set of dashboards that show what is creating real pipeline, meaning deals a salesperson is actually working, and what only looks busy. Whatever we learn there goes straight back into the top of the machine: better lists, sharper signals, messages that sound more like a person wrote them.

A real week

That’s the theory. In practice a week is messier, and more interesting. Last week began with a bug: a third of our new leads were arriving without a job title. It turned out two data providers were overwriting each other, and the fix was nothing more than changing the order they run in. On Tuesday I built an alert that fires when a company we care about posts a job ad for a role we sell to, and sends it to the account’s owner with the context attached.

On Wednesday I didn’t build anything. I sat in on four sales calls instead, because you can’t build for a team you don’t watch work. Thursday was cleanup. I deleted forty-one CRM fields that nobody was using, and nobody noticed, which was the point. On Friday I went through the weekly numbers with our head of sales, and we killed a campaign that had looked busy for a month and booked nothing.

What it isn’t

It isn’t classic sales operations, though it grew out of it. Sales ops traditionally keeps the tools configured and the reports running. A GTM engineer is expected to build: to write code, design how data flows, ship automations and own whether they work. It isn’t quite data engineering either, because success isn’t a tidy stream of data. Success is a salesperson having a better conversation on Thursday than they would have had without you.

And it isn’t spam at scale. The worst version of this job is someone who wires up a tool to send ten thousand generic emails. The best version uses automation to make outreach more relevant, not more frequent.

What you need to be good at it

You need enough code to be dangerous. For most people that means scripting, SQL and being comfortable with APIs. You don’t have to be a software engineer, but you do have to build things yourself instead of filing a ticket and waiting.

You need data instincts, which mostly means noticing a number is wrong before anyone tells you. You need sales empathy, an honest sense of what a rep’s day feels like, because the things you build only matter if they get used. And you need taste in tools, which is knowing when to buy, when to build and, more often than you’d think, when to delete.

If you’re curious about the role, or you’re already doing it and see it differently, I’d love to hear from you. That’s half the reason I write these.
